A B S T R A C T
Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) embed governing differential equations into training, enabling so­
lutions of ODEs and PDEs. This review consolidates theoretical foundations (expressive capacity, automatic 
differentiation) and core methods (loss design, constraint enforcement, sampling, optimization), while surveying 
applications from baseline PINNs to advanced variants such as multi-physics coupling, domain decomposition, 
frequency-enhanced representations, and operator-learning hybrids.
Comparative synthesis links architectural and training choices to equation type, data conditions, and com­
putational budgets. A unified benchmarking framework is proposed with standard PDE tasks, accuracy metrics, 
collocation budgets, and transparent reporting for fair comparison with classical solvers.
Evidence positions PINNs as complementary to traditional methods-effective for inverse problems, data as­
similation, irregular domains, and parametric inference-while challenges remain in scalability, spectral bias, 
constraint enforcement, and reproducibility. The review offers a coherent synthesis with actionable guidance 
for scientific and engineering applications.
1 . Introduction
1.1 . Background and motivation
The intersection of artificial intelligence and computational physics 
has witnessed rapid advances in recent decades, transforming ap­
proaches to complex mathematical modelling. Classical numerical 
solvers, despite their maturity, struggle with high dimensionality, in­
verse problems, and data-scarce regimes, motivating learning-based 
alternatives [18,54]. Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs), intro­
duced systematically by Raissi et al. [139,140], integrate physical laws 
with deep learning architectures to overcome these limitations. By em­
bedding governing differential equations into neural network training 
via customized loss functions, PINNs leverage universal approximation 
while enforcing physical constraints, yielding solutions that are both 
data-driven and physics-consistent.
PINNs represent a shift from conventional numerical approaches 
by embedding governing differential equations directly into the neu­
ral network training process through customized loss functions. This 
innovative framework leverages the universal approximation capabili­
ties of neural networks while enforcing physical constraints, enabling 
solutions that are data-driven and physics-consistent [ 34]. Unlike tra­
ditional machine learning approaches that require extensive labelled 
datasets, PINNs can operate in an unsupervised manner, learning so­
lutions by minimizing residuals of governing equations, boundary 
conditions, and initial conditions [ 100]. By utilizing separable neu­
ral networks to decompose high-dimensional input spaces into smaller, 
manageable sub-problems, [ 107] introduced S-PI-DeepONet, thereby 
significantly reducing the number of required trainable parameters. To 
predict microstructural mechanical responses across varying periodic 
domains, [56] introduced SPiFOL, which integrates Spectral methods 
and Finite Operator Learning with physics-informed losses to lever­
age frequency-domain representations. A linearized L1-type compact 
difference scheme, providing rigorous proofs for discrete energy stabil­
ity and the maximum principle within 2D systems, is utilized in [ 21], 
while quantum search mechanisms have been integrated into evolu­
tionary algorithms for optimizing continuum structure topology designs 
[184]. PINNs have also been applied to enhance out-of-distribution 
generalization for real-time weld expulsion inspection tasks [ 190], 
a Brooks–Iyengar quantum Byzantine Agreement-centered blockchain 
model has been proposed to secure smart healthcare IoT networks [210],

Building on this systematic analysis of PINN surveys from 2018 to 
2026, the present review identifies persistent conceptual and method­
ological gaps. Prior surveys emphasize foundational formulations, se­
lected domains, or isolated advances, often presenting problem classes 
in a fragmented manner that obscures methodological distinctions. In 
contrast, this work provides a structured synthesis by organizing PINN 
methodologies according to problem characteristics—ordinary differen­
tial equations, classical PDEs, high-dimensional systems, and emerging 
fractional-order models. Beyond this structural gap, limited critical 
discussion exists on scalability, efficiency, benchmarking, and the tran­
sition from proof-of-concept to engineering-grade applications, all of 
which are systematically addressed here. The novelty of this review lies 
in its critical and quantitative synthesis of advances targeting practi­
cal limitations, including scalability to high-dimensional spatiotemporal 
systems, computational efficiency, and the role of physics-informed 
operator learning. By integrating architectural analysis, variant cate­
gorization, benchmarking practices, and underrepresented real-world 
applications such as medical modelling, traffic flow, and climate fore­
casting, this work provides a forward-looking roadmap for deploying 
PINNs as engineering-grade computational tools—an integrative per­
spective largely absent from prior surveys.
1.2 . Evolution from traditional numerical methods
The conceptual roots of using neural networks for solving differ­
ential equations trace back to early studies by Lagaris et al. [ 82] and 
Dissanayake and Thien [41], which demonstrated mesh-free neural ap­
proximations for boundary value problems. While pioneering, these 
efforts were limited by weak theoretical rigor and the absence of efficient 
automatic differentiation, now recognized as essential for enforcing 
differential equation constraints [
17]. The advent of deep learning 
frameworks and scalable automatic differentiation subsequently enabled 
exact derivative computation, accelerating progress in this direction 
[
128].
The modern formulation of Physics-Informed Neural Networks 
(PINNs) was crystallized through the seminal contributions of Raissi and 
co-workers [
139,140], who introduced a unified framework capable of 
addressing both forward and inverse problems. By embedding govern­
ing equations directly into training, PINNs departed from traditional 
discretization-based solvers and enabled data-integrated modelling, pa­
rameter identification, and discovery-driven scientific computing [
30]. 
As illustrated in Fig. 1 , this paradigm emphasizes mesh-free com­
putation, seamless incorporation of observational data, and explicit 
enforcement of physical laws.
Subsequent developments have expanded PINNs to nonlinear PDEs, 
multiscale phenomena, and operator-learning paradigms, further con­
solidating their role as a central methodology in scientific machine 
learning. In many contexts, PINNs now complement and even extend 
classical numerical approaches [
92,99].
1.3 . Scope and objectives
Despite rapid growth and promising applications, the PINN field 
still lacks a comprehensive systematic review that not only surveys 
applications but also critically examines methodological advances, the­
oretical foundations, and unresolved challenges. Existing reviews often 
focus narrowly on specific domains or provide broad overviews with­
out sufficient methodological depth [
23,52]. This review addresses that 
gap by systematically examining recent developments in PINNs for 
solving ordinary and partial differential equations, with emphasis on 
methodological innovations, limitations, and future directions. Studies 
were identified through major databases (Scopus, Web of Science, Google 
Scholar) covering 2018 to 2025, prioritizing peer-reviewed journals, 
complemented by select IEEE conferences and relevant non-indexed 
sources. Inclusion criteria required relevance to PINN methodology, 
differential equation applications, and availability of quantitative or 
comparative performance data.
We explicitly highlight the novelty and significance of this review 
compared with existing surveys. While earlier reviews [23,34,63,77,84,
102,143,209] mainly offered descriptive summaries or domain-specific 
discussions, the present work uniquely provides a systematic, cross-type 
perspective encompassing all major differential equation classes (ODEs 
and PDEs) solved using PINNs and their advanced variants. Key con­
tributions include coverage of methodological advances, benchmarking 
aspects, and quantitative comparisons. Furthermore, the review compre­
hensively discusses existing PINN variants, explaining their theoretical 
motivation, comparative advantages over classical numerical methods, 
and current limitations.

2.2 . Universal approximation and expressive power of neural networks
Since PINNs approximate the solution 𝑢(𝑥, 𝑡) of a differential equation 
with a neural network 𝑢𝜃(𝑥, 𝑡), it is important to establish the theoret­
ical foundation that justifies this choice. The Universal Approximation 
Theorem provides this justification by showing that neural networks are 
capable of representing a wide class of functions.
Classical results by Cybenko [ 35] and Hornik et al. [61] demon­
strated that a feedforward neural network with a single hidden layer 
and finitely many neurons can approximate any continuous function on 
a compact subset of R𝑛 to arbitrary accuracy, provided the activation 
function is non-constant, bounded, and monotonically increasing. More 
precisely, for any continuous function 𝑓 ∶ [0, 1]𝑛 → R and any 𝜖 > 0, 
there exists a neural network N (𝑥) such that

For PINNs, this theorem is particularly relevant because solutions 
to well-posed differential equations are typically smooth functions that 
satisfy the continuity assumptions of the theorem [60]. In practice, how­
ever, shallow networks often require an impractically large number of 
neurons, motivating the use of deeper architectures.
Recent advances in approximation theory provide a stronger justi­
fication for the deep networks typically employed in PINNs. Lu et al. 
[101] showed that deep ReLU networks can efficiently approximate 
functions with compositional structures, while Yarotsky [ 196] estab­
lished improved approximation rates for deep networks compared to 
shallow ones. These results highlight the expressive power of deep ar­
chitectures, explaining their success in capturing the complex solution 
spaces of PDEs and ODEs.
Importantly, the approximation capabilities of neural networks ex­
tend beyond functions to their derivatives. Han et al. [54] demonstrated 
that neural networks can approximate not only functions but also their 
spatial and temporal derivatives, which is essential for PINNs since 
the governing physics constraints directly involve differential opera­
tors. Introduced the Zero Coordinate Shift (ZCS) technique, a modified 
automatic differentiation strategy that eliminates coordinate shifts dur­
ing derivative calculations within physics-informed operator learning 
frameworks was introduced in [87].
2.3 . Automatic differentiation and its role in PINNs
A key enabler of the PINNs methodology is the efficient and exact 
computation of derivatives required to evaluate differential operators. 
Traditional numerical differentiation methods, such as finite differences, 
introduce discretization and truncation errors that can accumulate and 
degrade solution accuracy [ 89]. In contrast, automatic differentiation
(AD) provides derivatives accurate up to machine precision, allow­
ing physics constraints to be enforced precisely during training. This 
capability is fundamental to the success of PINNs.
Automatic differentiation operates by decomposing a complex func­
tion into a sequence of elementary operations and applying the chain 
rule systematically. For a composite function:

Modern deep learning frameworks such as PyTorch [121] and

Navier–Stokes equations involve mixed partial derivatives and nonlinear 
terms [179].
2.4 
. Loss function formulation
The 
construction of the physics-informed loss function is central to 
the performance of PINNs. While the general formulation has been in­
troduced in Section 2.1, the practical success of PINNs strongly depends 
on how this loss is weighted, how collocation points are sampled, and 
how the resulting optimization landscape is navigated [105,191]. In this 
section, we review methodological advances in these three aspects.

Uniform sampling.  Distributes collocation points evenly across the 
computational domain, ensuring consistent coverage; however, it often 
under-resolves localized sharp gradients or multiscale features, leading 
to inefficient use of training points in large or heterogeneous domains 
[185].
Adaptive sampling.
 Concentrates collocation points in regions where 
the current solution exhibits large residuals or steep variations, thereby 
improving local accuracy at the expense of additional computational 
overhead associated with residual evaluation and dynamic point reallo­
cation [123]. While effective for low- to moderate-dimensional PDEs on 
continuous Euclidean domains, its scalability to large-scale or graph-
structured problems remains challenging, as residuals defined over 
graph Laplacians may be nonlocal and expensive to evaluate globally. 
When scaling PINNs to city-scale road networks, major computational 
bottlenecks arise from the large graph size, nonlocal residual evalua­
tion over graph Laplacians, increased automatic differentiation cost, and 
slow convergence caused by highly heterogeneous network dynamics 
(e.g., traffic flow on large networks) [64,167].
Quasi-random sampling.
 Such as low-discrepancy Sobol sequences, 
which improve domain coverage compared to purely random sam­
pling while maintaining computational efficiency [163]. Nevertheless, 
these approaches still face limitations in capturing complex topolog­
ical structures inherent in non-Euclidean domains, highlighting the 
need for graph-aware or topology-adaptive sampling strategies in large 
spatiotemporal systems.
2.4.3 . Optimization landscape and training challenges
The PINN loss function is inherently nonconvex, presenting signifi­
cant optimization challenges. Recent theoretical advances have begun 
to characterize its landscape and convergence properties.
Wang et al. [178] demonstrated that neural networks exhibit a 
spectral bias, favouring low-frequency components over high-frequency 
ones. This bias can hinder accurate resolution of multiscale solutions, 
motivating Fourier-feature embeddings and specialized architectures 
[161]. Shin et al. [153] established convergence results for PINNs on 
linear elliptic PDEs, deriving error bounds under specific regularity 
assumptions.
A further challenge is the curse of dimensionality. While PINNs 
show promise for high-dimensional PDEs, rigorous convergence guar­
antees remain limited, and ongoing work continues to refine theoretical 
understanding [65,71].
Together, these insights form the mathematical and computational 
backbone of PINNs, bridging deep learning with scientific computing.
3 . Core PINNs architecture and methodology
This section details the baseline PINN pipeline Fig. 2, focusing on 
(i) network components, (ii) activation choices, (iii) physics integration 
(collocation and residual evaluation), and (iv) training and optimiza­
tion. Unlike generic deep learning setups, PINNs pair a neural network 
with a physics-informed loss and residual evaluation via automatic 
differentiation (cf. Section 2). We adopt the fully connected (feedfor­
ward) backbone as the reference design, noting variants only where they 
materially affect PDE performance.
As established in Section 2.2, neural networks are universal 
function approximators [ 19,35,196]. In practice, however, identify­
ing weights/biases that solve a particular PDE remains challenging 
[176], and choosing an appropriate architecture is problem dependent. 
Broadly, one distinguishes between shallow networks (a single hidden 
layer) and deep networks (multiple layers) [4], with many architectural 
families surveyed in the literature [ 4,122,150]. For PINNs, fully con­
nected feedforward networks (FFNNs / FNNs / FCNNs) are the standard 
baseline; other families like CNNs and RNNs [27,85], and modern vari­
ants such as autoencoders (AE), deep belief networks (DBNs), generative 
adversarial networks (GANs), and Bayesian deep learning (BDL) [8,19] 
are used selectively when they offer concrete advantages (e.g., feature 
extraction, latent encodings, uncertainty quantification). An overview 
flowchart of the PINN model is provided in Fig. 2 and discussed below.

The output layer is the final layer of an MLP. Here, in Fig. 2, the

and IC losses, summed into the total loss. If convergence is poor, further 
optimization is applied until the PINN solution approaches the true PDE 
solution. The process is repeated until the model output is closer to the 
true solution.

3.2.1 . Design criteria and common choices
Key criteria for selecting activations in PINNs include: (i) differen­
tiability class, ensuring stable first- and second-order derivatives; (ii) 
mitigation of spectral bias, enabling representation of high-frequency 
components; (iii) suitability for the underlying problem structure (os­
cillatory, advective, diffusive); and (iv) optimization stability, avoiding 
vanishing or exploding gradients. Representative activation functions 
are summarized in Table 3.1 and discussed below.
Tanh. Commonly used in baseline PINNs for their smoothness and sta­
ble higher-order derivatives; a robust default for diffusion-dominated 
PDEs [75]. Training may slow down due to saturation, mitigated by 
careful initialization and learning-rate scheduling.
ReLU / leaky-ReLU. Piecewise linear with discontinuous first deriva­
tive and zero second derivative almost everywhere. This limits accuracy 
when PDE residuals require higher-order derivatives, making ReLU 
generally unsuitable as the sole activation in PINNs [2].
SoftPlus, swish / SiLU, GELU. Smooth alternatives that address ReLU’s 
derivative limitations while maintaining favourable optimization prop­
erties. Effective when tanh saturates, though their higher-order deriva­
tives can become stiff in certain PDEs [109].
Periodic / oscillatory activations.  For wave- or Helmholtz-type PDEs, 
periodic activations (e.g., sine) or frequency-enhanced inputs improve 
representation of oscillatory solutions and mitigate spectral bias [178]. 
Proper initialization and scaling are required to maintain gradient 
stability.
Fourier feature encodings. Fixed random Fourier mappings applied to 
inputs before standard activations expand frequency bandwidth and 
counteract low-frequency bias [161]. Effective for multiscale fields, with 
frequencies tuned to the PDE’s characteristic scales.
3.2.2 . Practical recommendations by PDE character
• Diffusion-dominated PDEs (e.g., heat): prefer smooth activations 
such as tanh or SoftPlus; tanh is a common default [161].
• Advection/reaction or sharp layers: use Swish or GELU; combine 
with adaptive sampling to resolve steep gradients.
• Oscillatory/wave/Helmholtz problems: employ periodic activa­
tions or Fourier features to capture high frequencies and mitigate 
spectral bias [161,178].
Smoother activations benefit from variance-preserving initialization; 
periodic activations and Fourier encodings require frequency scaling 
tuned to the domain size to avoid vanishing/exploding gradients. These 
choices interact with collocation sampling and loss weighting.

3.3.3 . Collocation scheduling and mini-batching
Accuracy and stability depend on collocation point strategies during 
training [1]:
• Mini-batching across residual types: sample small batches from 
interior ( Ω), boundary ( 𝜕Ω), and initial sets to balance gradient 
contributions.
• Adaptive resampling: periodically resample points with probability 
proportional to residual magnitude, focusing capacity on difficult 
regions (layers, shocks, oscillations).
• Quasi-random coverage: employ low-discrepancy sequences (e.g., 
Sobol) for baseline coverage, augmented with adaptive refinement 
for efficiency.
3.3.4 . Normalization and nondimensionalization
Normalizing inputs/outputs and nondimensionalizing the governing 
equations improve conditioning and balance residual scales. In practice, 
coordinates are standardized to [0,1] 𝑑 (or zero-mean, unit-variance) and 
target fields are scaled so that PDE, BC, and IC residual magnitudes are 
comparable before weighting [127].
The remainder of this section details the optimization and train­
ing procedure, which operationalizes residual evaluation and constraint 
enforcement.
3.4 . Training optimization framework
PINN training couples neural optimization with physics-driven objec­
tives. A robust strategy is to begin with a stochastic first-order optimizer 
(e.g., Adam) to explore the loss landscape, then switch to a quasi-Newton 
method (e.g., L-BFGS) once convergence plateaus [79,166]. L-BFGS per­
forms best in (near) full-batch mode; for very large collocation sets, 
Adam with residual-focused mini-batches remains preferable. Learning-
rate control is critical: start conservatively ( 10−3 − 10 −4), reduce on 
plateaus via schedulers (step, plateau, cosine), and monitor per-term 
trends to avoid stalling higher-order derivatives or destabilizing BC/IC 
residuals.
Batching and sampling must balance gradient contributions from 
interior, boundary, initial, and optional data residuals. Mini-batching 
across residual types prevents dominance by any single term, while 
residual-based resampling concentrates points in high-error regions (lay­
ers, shocks, oscillations) and preserves coverage via low-discrepancy 
sequences. Normalization and nondimensionalization improve condi­
tioning: standardize inputs (e.g., map coordinates to [0,1] 𝑑 or zero 
mean/unit variance) and scale outputs so PDE, BC, IC, and data resid­
uals remain comparable before weighting. Loss balancing should be 
revisited after changes in sampling or scaling, using fixed, adaptive, or 
gradient-based weights (cf. 
Section 2.4).
Regularization and stabilization must be physics-aware. Prefer early 
stopping based on held-out physics metrics (interior residual norms, 
BC/IC violations) rather than training loss alone; apply gradient clip­
ping when higher-order derivatives spike; add light weight decay if 
needed. For oscillatory or multiscale solutions, periodic activations or 
Fourier-feature encodings (Section 3.2) mitigate spectral bias and ease 
optimization. Mixed-precision can accelerate training but requires verifi­
cation of accuracy for second and mixed derivatives. Diagnostics should 
track per-term residuals, validation residuals on independent colloca­
tion sets, constraint violations, and optimizer state (learning rate, step 
acceptance for L-BFGS). True convergence is indicated by simultane­
ous reduction of interior and constraint residuals; low training loss with 
high held-out residuals typically signals sampling or weighting issues. 
Practical default: Use Adam with a decaying learning rate and mini-
batched residuals, augmented by adaptive resampling. Transition to 
full-batch L-BFGS for refinement once Adam plateaus. Normalize in­
puts/outputs and rebalance residual weights after any resampling or 
scaling adjustments.

4 . PINNs applications to ODEs
Unlike the PDE setting, ODE problems use a one-dimensional (typi­
cally temporal) domain, with initial conditions playing the primary role; 
boundary terms appear only in boundary-value ODEs. Consequently, 
PINN collocation is along time (and parameters, when present), and 
convergence is often sensitive to stiffness and step-scale. The prior re­
search work is grouped by ODE characteristics like solution technique, 
computational budget, strengths & weaknesses, practical experience, & 
architecture in Table (Table 4.1). A summary of existing work on ODEs 
solved through PINNs is illustrated in Fig. 3. 
From the quantitative results in Table 4.1, several performance pat­
terns emerge. For diffusion-dominated and smooth temporal systems, 
standard PINNs with tanh or SoftPlus activations provide stable and ac­
curate convergence even with moderate collocation sets. Stiff ODEs, in 
contrast, expose optimization instability and sensitivity to learning-rate 
schedules; adaptive weighting and transfer-learning variants improve 
training efficiency by 20–40%. In fast–slow and parametric systems, 
deeper networks and residual-based resampling yield more balanced 
gradients and lower 𝐿2 errors. These behaviours show that computa­
tional cost and accuracy are highly dependent on the equation type, 
data quality, and architectural design. Overall, the quantitative com­
parisons highlight that PINN performance is not uniform but governed 
by the interplay of PDE/ODE character, noise levels, and computational 
budgets.
5 . PINNs applications to PDEs
Partial differential equations extend the ODE setting by introduc­

on 𝜕Ω together with mixed and higher-order derivatives. In the PINN

entiation, and boundary/initial constraints are enforced either softly 
(penalties) or by hard architectural embeddings. This mesh-free, data-
integrated approach is particularly attractive on irregular domains, in 
inverse/parameter-identification tasks, and when only sparse or noisy

7 . Computational assessment, benchmarking, and limitations
Having surveyed baseline PINNs and advanced variants 
(Sections 2 –6), we summarize practical evaluation guidance and 
key limitations.
Benchmarking and reporting. We establish a structured benchmarking 
framework grounded in standard PDE test cases, including the Poisson, 
heat, Burgers’, and Navier–Stokes equations (at fixed Reynolds num­
bers). The framework requires explicit disclosure of collocation budgets 
and sampling strategies, clear separation of training and validation 
collocation sets, and consistent reporting of both PDE residuals and 
physics-aware metrics such as boundary and initial condition (BC/IC) 
violations and invariant preservation.
For comparisons with traditional numerical solvers (e.g., FDM, FEM, 
FVM), the framework mandates alignment of PDE formulations, BC/IC 
specifications, and data conditions, alongside comprehensive reporting 
of computational budgets for both paradigms (assembly/solve versus 
training/inference) [75,89,100,140].
To ensure reproducibility and comparability, the benchmarking pro­
tocol defines fixed PDE tasks, standardized data partitions, explicit 
accuracy metrics (e.g., 𝐿2, BC/IC errors), and systematic reporting of 
computational costs and robustness across multiple runs. This structured 
framework replaces informal methodological advice with a formalized, 
measurable, and comparable benchmarking standard consistent with 
emerging PINN evaluation practices.
Computational cost and scaling.  Cost grows with collocation size, 
depth/width, and derivative order; higher-order AD is often domi­
nant [ 17,128]. Mixed precision can help but must be validated for 
second/mixed derivatives. Scalability improves with domain decompo­
sition (cPINN/XPINN) and residual-type mini-batches [69,155].
Stability, failure modes, mitigations.  Common issues include spec­
tral (low-frequency) bias and multiscale behaviour [ 136,178], stiff­
ness/sharp fronts, and loss imbalance causing BC/IC leakage [ 81]. 
Remedies: periodic/Fourier features [161], residual-based adaptive sam­
pling/curricula [100,123], normalization/nondimensionalization [75], 
and domain decomposition [ 69,155]. Report not only final errors but 
also failure rates and sensitivity to seeds/hyperparameters [81].
Limitations.
• Complex Domain: despite their flexibility, PINNs often strug­
gle when applied to complex or irregular domains. Challenges 
arise from inaccurate boundary-condition enforcement, geometry-
induced anisotropy, and imbalance among loss terms, which can 
lead to unstable training and slow convergence. These difficul­
ties become more pronounced in highly non-uniform geometries 
and multiphysics settings, limiting the reliability of standard PINN 
formulations without geometry-aware transformations or domain-
adaptive strategies [62,132].
• Optimization complexity: nonconvex objectives; failures often 
optimization-driven rather than expressivity-limited [81].
• Compute/memory: higher-order AD and large collocation sets are 
costly [17,128].
• Spectral bias/multiscale: difficulty capturing high frequencies and 
sharp features [136,178].
• Constraint enforcement: soft penalties may under-enforce BC/IC 
without careful weighting or hard constraints.
• Theory: convergence/error guarantees exist only for selected 
classes; still less mature than for classical discretizations [38,153].
• Problem classes: classical PINNs struggle on integro-differential 
forms; auxiliary outputs (A-PINNs) can help [199].

7.1 . Future directions
Building upon the limitations identified in Sections 5 –6 (spectral 
bias, stiffness, scalability, and uncertainty), the following research di­
rections outline concrete pathways toward addressing these challenges.
Significant progress has been made in leveraging PINNs across in­
terdisciplinary domains. While this study has focused on PINNs for 
differential equations, several challenges and open questions remain. 
Advancing the field will require both methodological innovations and 
broader applications that push beyond current limitations [75,140].
Translational applications. Representative studies have applied PINNs 
to cerebrovascular diffusion MRI [ 172], brain hemodynamics [ 147], 
3D blood flow modelling [ 119], and coronary artery trees [ 9], clini­
cal medicine [ 16], injection-molded parts [ 49], aortic blood flow [ 2], 
while surveys highlight broader imaging tasks [13]. Extensions include 
myocardial perfusion quantification ([168], MR electrical properties to­
mography [66], defect detection with physics-guided constraints [ 49], 
and breast ultrasound segmentation [ 40]. However, existing methods 
still face limitations in scalability, generalization across patient-specific 
anatomies, and clinical reliability.
Future work could prioritize scalable PINN variants with adaptive 
physics weighting, uncertainty quantification, and hybrid integration 
of low-latency surrogates and task-specific feature extractors, enabling 
faster inference and uncertainty-aware personalization across patient 
geometries. Standardized clinical benchmarks would further ensure 
physically consistent and statistically reliable predictions, supporting 
robust surgical and diagnostic deployment.
Urban infrastructure and traffic flow. Recent research has demonstrated 
the effectiveness of physics-informed neural networks for traffic state es­
timation and flow modelling by embedding traffic conservation laws and 
physically derived flow equations into learning frameworks [167,195]. 
These approaches enable accurate reconstruction of traffic states from 
sparse sensor data and connected-vehicle information, while adaptive 
and residual PINN formulations further improve robustness and gener­
alization across different traffic networks [160,173]. However, existing 
methods still face challenges in scaling to large, heterogeneous trans­
portation systems and in capturing rapidly changing traffic dynamics.
Subsequent research could focus on developing scalable and trans­
ferable PINN architectures that integrate adaptive physics constraints, 
multi-modal traffic data fusion, dynamic topology handling, and non-
oscillatory shock-capturing mechanisms to ensure numerical stability 
near traffic discontinuities, alongside federated learning frameworks 
for cross-city knowledge transfer with privacy preservation, enabling 
reliable real-time traffic prediction and control.

lished PINNs as effective tools for engineering applications, including

Operator learning and neural operators.
 Recent advances in operator 
learning and physics-informed modelling have enabled data-driven ap­

yet challenges persist in integrating operator-learning with physics-

Optimization and training stability.
 Contemporary studies have pro­
posed improved training strategies for PINNs by enforcing temporal 
causality, conservative constraints, and domain decomposition to en­
hance stability and convergence, particularly for nonlinear and time-
dependent differential equations [69,155,178]. Complementary ad­

diffusion equations, Fourier feature embeddings for capturing high-

DeepXDE, have further broadened the practical applicability of PINNs 
[11,100,161]. These studies successfully stabilize training for multi-
scale problems via physical constraints and parallelized domain par­
titioning, yet challenges persist in robust handling of stiff systems, 
multi-scale dynamics, and complex geometries.
Emerging directions should develop unified training frameworks 
merging causality-aware learning, adaptive spectral/Fourier feature 
mapping, structure-preserving constraints, and automated weight sched­
ulers. These bridge local decomposition with global consistency, en­
abling asynchronous parallel PINNs for scalable, stable performance 
across diverse PDE classes on large-scale GPU clusters without interface 
bottlenecks.

 Current re­

harmonize physical modelling with deep learning to improve inter­
pretability, data efficiency, and predictive reliability across materials’ 
science, vehicle dynamics, structural identification, and fatigue-life es­
timation [24,36,55,213 ]. By embedding physical estimators, damage 
mechanics, and interval uncertainty representations, these approaches 
outperform purely data-driven models, particularly under limited or 
noisy data conditions. Recent studies have advanced beyond black-box 
models by embedding damage mechanics and interval analysis into 
training, enabling accurate life prediction with sparse, uncertain data—
yet current methods remain problem-specific, lacking unified principles 
and scalability.
Future work should develop generalizable physics–data fusion archi­
tectures integrating Phase-Field modelling for multiscale crack nucle­

RUL (remaining useful life) estimation. Modular frameworks will en­
sure computational efficiency, interpretability, and systematic domain 
physics integration across diverse engineering systems like aerospace 
and automotive.
Neural architectures for spatiotemporal and PDE-driven learning.
 Recent 
advances have expanded physics-informed learning through structured 
neural architectures, including convolutional–recurrent networks for 
spatiotemporal PDEs, graph neural Galerkin formulations for forward 
and inverse problems, and first-order PINN formulations that improve 
numerical stability and training efficiency [46,47,142]. These develop­
ments align with broader physics-informed machine-learning principles 
that emphasize structure preservation, interpretability, and data effi­
ciency [75], while related applications demonstrate the potential of 
hybrid learning in complex event modelling [12]. Recent works have 
successfully extended PINNs to non-Euclidean geometries and long-term 
dependencies via recurrence and graph theory in loss minimization—yet 
challenges persist in unifying architectures under a common framework 
for large-scale, multi-physics systems.
Future research could develop modular, structure-aware PINN vari­
ants combining graph, recurrent, first-order formulations, and Physics-
Informed Graph Transformers with symbolic regression. These enable 
scalable spatiotemporal modelling, computational scaling of graph 
solvers, automated law discovery, and real-time correction of incomplete 
physics for long-range multi-scale interactions.
Multi-scale forecasting and image processing.
 Emerging investigations 
highlight the growing impact of PINNs in forecasting and image pro­
cessing, including spatiotemporal production forecasting via graph-
based PINNs, fractional and time-varying volatility modelling, hybrid

and physics-constrained wave forecasting using dispersion relations 
[31,74,96,174,202]. In parallel, PINNs have been successfully applied 
to image processing tasks such as digital image correlation and phase 
imaging by embedding governing physical laws directly into reconstruc­

equations into neural architectures for physically consistent forecasts 
and image reconstructions under noisy data—yet current approaches 
remain task-specific, limited in long-horizon uncertainty, multi-scale 
dynamics, and cross-domain generalization.
Future work could integrate PINNs with LSTM/Transformers, 
Physics-Informed Diffusion Models, ViT-PINN hybrids, frac­

and vision applications.
8 . Conclusions
This review synthesizes advances in Physics-Informed Neural 
Networks (PINNs), integrating theory, methodology, comparative anal­
yses, and emerging directions. By classifying recent work across ODEs 
and PDEs, it highlights how architectural and optimization choices shape 
accuracy, stability, and efficiency, with consistent performance patterns 
across elliptic, parabolic, hyperbolic, nonlinear, and fractional systems.
A key contribution is a reproducible benchmarking framework spec­
ifying standardized tests, metrics, and disclosure protocols to ensure 
transparency and comparability. Persistent challenges remain-spectral 
bias, stiffness, scalability, constraint enforcement, and reproducibility 
gaps.
Future progress will rely on adaptive sampling, hybrid operator 
learning, and physics-guided embeddings to enhance generalization 
and efficiency. Benchmark-driven reproducibility, uncertainty quantifi­
cation, and open data sharing are essential for PINNs to mature into 
scalable, reliable tools for complex differential equations and interdisci­
plinary innovation.

Author biography
Mr. Subarna Khanra is a Research Scholar in the Department 
of Mathematics at SLIET Longowal, India. His research fo­
cuses on the development and analysis of numerical methods 
for solving PDEs. In particular, he is interested in leveraging 
PINNs and other ML based computational frameworks to ob­
tain efficient solutions of complex differential models arising 
in applied mathematics, physics, and engineering systems.
Dr. Vijay Kumar Kukreja is a Professor in the Department 
of Mathematics at SLIET, Longowal, India. He specializes 
in advanced numerical methods for differential equations, 
with broad research interests encompassing applied mathe­
matics, numerical solutions of PDEs, BVPs, fluid flow through 
porous media, mathematical modelling, and data-driven com­
putational techniques. Dr. Kukreja has published numerous 
research papers in internationally reputed journals and has 
presented his work at several prestigious conferences. He has 
also served as a Guest Editor for two books and two special 
issues published by Springer and MDPI, respectively. His on­
going work focuses on integrating modern numerical analysis with emerging areas of data 
science and scientific machine learning.
Dr. Indu Bala is an Assistant Professor in the School of 
Computer Science and Information Technology at Adelaide 
University, Australia. Her research integrates applied math­
ematics, data science, and artificial intelligence to develop 
innovative solutions for complex real-world challenges. She 
works at the intersection of optimisation, stochastic mod­
elling, and nature-inspired computational algorithms, with 
particular emphasis on applications in NLP and LLMs for 
healthcare and sustainability. Dr. Bala actively collaborates 
with international research groups and industry partners, fos­
tering multidisciplinary approaches to problem-solving. Her 
work focuses on designing transparent, interpretable, and responsible AI systems in power 
systems, water resource management, and healthcare innovation.
