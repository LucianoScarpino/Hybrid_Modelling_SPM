Abstract: In the context of hybrid twins, a data-driven enrichment is added to the physics-
based solution to represent with higher accuracy the reference solution assumed to be
known at different points in the physical domain. Such an approach enables better predic-
tions. However, the data-driven enrichment is usually represented by a regression, whose
main drawbacks are (i) the difficulty of understanding the subjacent physics and (ii) the
risks induced by the data-driven model extrapolation. This paper proposes a procedure
enabling the extraction of a differential operator associated with the enrichment provided
by the data-driven regression. For that purpose, a sparse Singular Value Decomposition,
SVD, is introduced. It is then employed, first, in a full operator representation regular-
ized optimization problem, where sparsity is promoted, leading to a linear programming
problem, and then in a tensor decomposition of the operator’s identification procedure.
The results show the ability of the method to identify the exact missing operators from the
model. The regularized optimization problem was also able to identify the weights of the
missing terms with a relative error of about 10% on average, depending on the selected
use case.
Keywords: scientific machine learning; model discovery; physics-informed neural networks;
PINN; sparse SVD

1. Introduction
Physics-based models make use of different differential operators, linear or nonlinear,
involving space and time. In what follows, and without loss of generality, we assume a
linear problem, defined in the domain Ω ⊂ R2. The points in Ω are noted by x, with com-
ponents (x, y). Thus, the problem can be expressed as
Lp(up(x)) = F(x), (1)
where Lp(·) represents the linear differential operator, the superscript •p, highlighting
the fact that the operator L comes from a state-of-the-art physics-based model, up(x) its
associated unknown field and F(x) the problem source term.
The solution of the problem results in the so-called physics-based model solutionup(x),
∀x ∈ Ω. Now, we assume that in different points x1, . . ., xP, the reference solution u(x) is
accessible, from appropriate and accurate measurements, offering the values u1, . . ., uP,
with ui ≡ u(xi), i = 1, . . . ,P.

First, a regression δθ(x) is constructed, where θ refers to the regression parameters.
The regression can be linear (e.g., regularized polynomial sparse Proper Generalized
Decomposition–sPGD) or nonlinear (e.g., a multi-layer perceptron neural network). The re-
gression training employs the available data (xi, δi), i = 1, . . . ,P.
Thus, the usual hybrid modeling proceeds as follows:
1. Define and solve the physics-based model: Lp(up(x)) = F → up(x).
2. From the available data, ( xi, δi), i = 1, . . . ,P, construct the regression δθ(x).
3. The solution could be expressed as the addition of both contributions, that is u(x) =
up(x) + δθ(x), where for the sake of notational simplicity the correction term will be
noted by uc(x), referring the field correction, emphasized by the superscript •c:
u(x) = up(x) + uc(x). (4)
Even if knowing uc(x) offers a clear advantage to improve the prediction accuracy, its
nature remains quite elusive, much less intuitive than the physics-based contribution. In
fact, up(x) results from a local physics expressed by the derivatives involved in the differ-
ential operator, whereas the regression uc(x) represents the effect of a hidden local physics.
Discovering a differential correction operator could be of major interest in transforming
ignorance into knowledge.
By introducing the reference solution u(x) given by Equation (4) into the physics-
based model, and taking into account the model linearity and the problem Definition (1),
the expression of the residual R(x) results
R(x) = −L p(up(x)) − Lp(uc(x)) + F = −L p(uc(x)). (5)
Now, it is assumed that a correction Lc(·) of the physics-based model Lp(·) must be
added, in such a way that
Lp(u(x)) + Lc(u(x)) = F, (6)
that, taking into account Equations (4) and (5), results in

which will be employed for discovering the linear or nonlinear differential correction

Unlike available techniques published in the literature, the aim of this work is to
identify the mathematical expression in the correction operator involved in the mapping of
the input forcing function towards the associated output. For example, the input function
can be mapped towards the output function through two nonlinear operators multiplying
each other [ 1,2]. However, this method does not derive an explicit expression of the
identified operator.
Other methods in the literature aim to approximate the differential operator as a
weighted sum of physical functions [3–5], while enforcing sparsity through regularization,
offering physically explainable results under the condition of including in the dictionary
the right functions. Moreover, classification techniques can be used to create a classifier,
which selects the best model to fit the measured data based on optimal trees, that performs
correctly as soon as the searched model is involved in the search dictionary [6]. In an effort
to enhance the predictability of the outputs as a function of the input variables variations,
different architectures can be proposed while considering any information coming from
existing physical laws [7]. However, all the proposed architectures aim to create a black
box form of the response without actually giving an explainable representation of the
physical model.
Differential operator discovery through a machine learning algorithm was recently
introduced in [8], where the neural network learns the approximation of an operator while
also generating a differential representation of the operator. The proposed strategy aims at
approximating at the same time the data points and the residual of the discovered operator
while applying regularization. The results prove the ability of the network to identify the
operator from a given library of differential operators after several hundreds of evaluations
of the solution. Differential equation discovery is also the subject of other works [9], where
Bayesian neural networks are used to identify the operator involved in the represented
physical problem.
Most of the aforementioned techniques aim to approximate the data response as a
whole and not the discrepancy between the results and the physical model. In fact, keeping
the physics-based model while enriching it enables explaining and certifying the resulting
final hybrid model [ 10]. In this sense, universal ordinary differential equations aim to
complement the physical operator with a neural one; however, they fail to give a physical
expression of the generated operator [11].
The present work aims to complement the physical operator with an explainable
enrichment (or correction). Moreover, the use of the coordinate separation allows for
fast identification of the operators without having to rely on a dictionary, allowing the
use of the total number of possible combinations of operators in each dimension. The
separated representation allows for fast computation and identification of the solution
without requiring numerous iterations of the full-order approximation. Moreover, the
proposed strategy accepts a tensor formulation that, as proved in many of our former
works, alleviates the curse of dimensionality. The enrichment operator is identified from the
physics-based model solution and its residual, both expressed in a separated representation.
The next section proposes a methodology for obtaining the residual separated repre-
sentation, a sort of sparse Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) [12–14], which circumvents
the dimensional limitations of the SVD and extends it beyond the classical High-Order
SVD (HOSVD) [15–17]. Later, Sections 3 and 4 will address the construction of the correc-
tion operator along with several examples, leveraging the separated representation just
discussed. The last section ends the manuscript with some conclusions.

One important question concerns the advantages of performing such a coordinates
separation instead of looking for a regression, ˆu(x, y) and ˆR(x, y), in the whole domain

approximation while offering the possibility of computing the different derivatives from
its automatic differentiation capabilities. In this work, we select the neural networks as
regressors for coordinate separation since they are commonly used as global approximators
of nonlinear functions. The main advantages of performing the coordinates separation are:
• The hierarchical approximation benefits, where the different scales of the coordi-
nate correlations are captured gradually. Thus, one could truncate and identify the
operators representing the most relevant contributions.
• When operating with a reduced amount of data, the separated representation al-
lows for the extraction of the first (most relevant) components of the decomposition,
whereas a monolithic approach exhibits less accuracy for the same amount of data.
• Separated representations facilitate the use of tensor decompositions, especially ap-
pealing when addressing highly multidimensional partial differential equations [22].

4.3. Discussion
The tensor formulation just presented is not designed to find the right coefficients
affecting the different derivatives composing the searched operator but to identify the
different derivatives involved in that search for differential operators. Because of the
iteration procedure, the different approximations involved in the separated representations,
and the regularizations with repeated and accumulated effects, one expects to capture the
significance of the involved derivatives more than computing accurately the coefficient
values affecting them.
Thus, as soon as the tensor formulation identifies the missing terms (derivatives), one
could consider the full-basis representation addressed in the previous section to calculate the
coefficients affecting the different derivatives involved in the enrichment differential operator.
To verify these expectations, the problem addressed in Section 3.3.1 is addressed again,
now by first employing the tensor formulation. The procedure was able to identify the
significance of the term representing ∂2
∂x∂y , which resulted in orders of magnitude higher
than the others, proving that the tensor formulation enables an efficient identification.
However, the tensor formulation failed to offer the right weight of the discovered term,
with a calculated value three orders of magnitude smaller than the reference one. The
tensor formulation is, therefore, suitable to detect the missing terms in the model, but it
is more convenient to use the approach described in Section 3 to find the corresponding

weights of the missing terms. However, the advantage of the tensor formulation is the
ability to use a large number of possible terms without increasing the computation time
required for the problem’s solution or evaluation.
Concerning the regularization parameters, it was noted that increasing the regular-
ization parameter helps in sparsity and identifying the exact missing terms in the PDE
but compromises the identification of the exact weights. Therefore, a suitable approach
would be to use a large regularization parameter for terms identification, followed by
another fitting, keeping only the identified terms from the PDE while selecting a low
regularization parameter.
From a computation time point of view, when using the least squares programming,
which is possible for both proposed strategies, the identification is quasi-instant as it only
involves inverting an m × m matrix, where m is the number of operators involved in
the approximation. Using a gradient descent algorithm would involve a slightly higher
computation time, as it involves an iterative gradient descent process. Nevertheless,
the identification time is around 120 s for all the proposed examples. The separated
representation approximation computation time depends heavily on the number of modes
involved in the approximation. This approximation required between three and five
minutes for convergence, for all the examples presented in this manuscript.
5. Conclusions
This paper proposed two different procedures for identifying the differential operators
representing the discrepancy between the solution (known at some locations) and the
physics-based solution.
First, the physics-based model solution and the discrepancy approximation are ex-
pressed in a separate form. Then, a dictionary of differential operators, or its associated
tensorial approximation, both regularized, are employed for identifying the most relevant
terms expected involved in the discrepancy operator.
The pertinence of the separated representations, as well as the regularized identifica-
tion procedure, were proved by a series of numerical tests.
