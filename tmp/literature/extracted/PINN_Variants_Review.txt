ABSTRACT
Physics- Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) are deep neural networks that incorporate partial differential equations in their loss 
function to understand the physics of the system. Thereby enhancing the learning process of the model to achieve better predic -
tive results than the existing models. PINNs embed governing physical principles directly into their loss functions, enabling them 
to solve complex problems even with limited or noisy data. This distinctive capability allows PINNs to effectively handle com -
plex computational and engineering problems. Such problems include fluid flow prediction, heat transfer simulation, and wave 
propagation analysis. Hence, a comprehensive analysis of PINNs and their variations for fluid dynamics in the human biological 
system will be presented in this work. The findings of this numerical evaluation indicate that PINN-  based models are always in 
agreement with reference and in vivo data, which proves to be more accurate in predicting the major hemodynamic parameters, 
including velocity, pressure, and wall shear stress (WSS). However, challenges including high computational cost, convergence 
instability, and limitations in multi-  scale generalization remain significant. Further, the review addresses the foundational ar -
chitecture of PINNs, improvements introduced through various extensions, and their relative advantages in different biofluid ap-
plications. Special emphasis will be placed on their applications in hemodynamics modeling blood flow dynamics and vascular 
biomechanics using PINNs. This study also discusses the major challenges of PINNs, such as high computational cost, conver -
gence issues, multi- scale modeling, and limited generalization. It also reviews proposed solutions and suggests future directions, 
including integration with other machine learning models for improved scalability and broader interdisciplinary applications.
1   |   Introduction
The problem of biofluid dynamics, and in particular cardiovas -
cular hemodynamics, was difficult to solve because of the com -
plexity of the interactions between fluid mechanics and vessel 
wall biomechanics, as well as patient-  specific anatomical vari -
ability (Berger and Malpas 1998; Bessonov et al.  2016; Tawhai and 
Burrowes  2008). The traditional methods such as computational 
fluid dynamics (CFD) and fluid–structure interaction (FSI) have 
been regularly used in the simulation of blood flow and vascular 
mechanics (Apraiz et al.  2024; Hu et al.  2021; Jiang et al.  2022; 
Q. Li et al.  2025; Y. Ling et al.  2022; Nakajima et al.  2022; Ning 
et al.  2023). Nevertheless, classical approaches demanded large 
mesh construction and a lot of computing forces, which were 
prohibitive in large-  scale or time-  dependent tasks of complex 
vascular geometries (Bateman et al.  2025; Candreva et al.  2022; 
Y. Ling et al.  2022). Moreover, their sensitivity to boundary and 
initial conditions often limited the applicability of the models

especially when clinical data were smaller and noisy (Kim and 
Koo  2025; Y. Ling et al.  2022; Zhao et al.  2021). These limitations 
made the calculations more complicated and required material 
property data that were hard to get in  vivo and of high fidel -

Reduced Order Models became available to provide simplicity in 
computations but a one- dimensional (1D) blood flow model ap -
plied reduced spatial resolution and spatial detail in flow and hy-
draulic representations of blood flow (Farias et al.  2023; Kissas 
et al.  2020). Equally, three- dimensional (3D) CFD resolved high- 
resolution flow configurations at a high computational rate in a 
way that usually made the idea of an inverse problem like param-
eter estimation or flow reconstruction using clinical measure -
ments practical (Oliveira et al.  2021; Yevtushenko et al.  2022). 
Traditional methods often encountered difficulties in modeling 
complex turbulence and were sensitive toward the boundary 
conditions with turbulent or transitional flows that were often 
present in pathological conditions such as stenosis or aneurysm 
(L. Chen et al.  2023; Mason et al.  2019; Misaki et al.  2021; Qiao 
et al.  2016; Steinman et al.  2002; Zhao et al.  2021). The short -
comings of these systems supported the urgency of developing 
a new architecture with a combination of basic principles of 
physics and clinical information that was not hindered compu -
tational performance.
Physics- informed neural networks (PINNs) have recently be -
come the new paradigm of computational modeling, explic -
itly using governing partial differential equations (PDEs) in 
the training of the neural network (Farea et  al.  2024; Sikora 
et al.  2024). Physics informed neural networks were neural net -
works that encode the physical laws typically partial differential 
equations, directly into their training process by including PDE 
residuals, boundary conditions, and initial conditions in the 
loss function by automatic differentiation (Bai et al.  2020; Singh 
et  al.  2024). This framework had seen a rapid evolution that 
made it applicable to many applications in computational phys -
ics, fluid dynamics and engineering systems. Recent articles 
have provided universal discussions of PINN algorithms, uses, 
research gaps, and the corresponding challenges. Particularly, 
a critical assessment of the fundamental methodological prin -
ciples of PINNs with the focus on the learning of physical laws 
in neural-  network training using physics-  based loss functions 
and using automatic differentiation to analyze the governing 
equation error. Contrary to purely data-  driven models, PINNs 
incorporated the residues of the PDEs, boundary conditions, 
and initial conditions in a composite loss minimization for -
mulation, hence imparting the network to seek physically 
consistent solutions even in case of sparse and noisy data (Liu 
et  al.  2025). This would use meshless methodologies that had 
not require discretisation and mesh generation and therefore 
allowed the accommodation of complex geometries and bound -
ary conditions with greater flexibility (Donnelly et al.  2024; Hu 
et al.  2021; Jo et al.  2024; Orera et al.  2025; Xu et al.  2023). The 
key mathematical idea behind the PINN was the automatic dif -
ferentiation of spatial and time derivatives, used to estimate the 
residues of the partial differential equations, which were then 
regarded as part of the loss function (Yuan et  al.  2022). The 
physics- informed regulariser confined the space of possible 
solutions to physically-  consistent states but further improved 
the generalization provided by solutions outside of the training 
data (Sarma et al.  2024).
The most recent methodological developments included the 
learning of boundary complexity progressively whereby the 
boundary conditions were progressively embedded slowly to 
stabilize the learning of complex vascular geometries (Wong 
et al.  2025). Also, hybrid methods based on surrogate modeling 
of boundary conditions, or parameter estimation, on PINNs de -
creased the cost of computing by using the strengths associated 
with each method (Alzhanov et al.  2024; Bateman et al.  2025; L. 
Chen et al.  2023) of these developments made PINNs a potent 
and excellent example of biofluid dynamics modeling. PINNs 
have been shown to be highly successful in the reconstruction 
of velocity and pressure fields at sparse clinical data including 
four- dimensional imaging (4D flow MRI) and Transcranial 
Doppler ultrasound (TCD) in the hemodynamics and vascu -
lar biomechanical context (Kissas et al.  2020). To use physics- 
informed archetypal neural networks (PINNs), as an example, 
they were shown to predict the wall shear stress (WSS) and pres-
sure gradients in patient-  specific types of arteries, with relative 
errors less when compared to a standard and CFD simulation (Y. 
Ling et al.  2022).
Physics- guided Neural Networks were the new trend in cardio -
vascular modeling that manages the shortcomings of traditional 
solutions by providing a powerful mathematical model, a high- 
quality optimisation, and novel methods of computational solu -
tions (Donnelly et al.  2024; Sarma et al.  2024; Sikora et al.  2024). 
The mathematical accuracy of successful PINN implementation 
involved the basic governing equations, complex constraint 
models, complicated optimisation algorithms, and multi-  scale 
integration measures. Such frameworks gave the basis to the ap-
plication of clinical translation and personalized medicine that 
likely to transform the cardiovascular diagnostics, treatment 
planning, and patient care application.
The objective of this systematic review was to present the founda-
tional architectures of PINNs and their methodological innova -
tions tailored to biofluid dynamics in human biological systems, 
with an emphasis on hemodynamics and vascular biomechan -
ics. This paper consists of nine sections with introduction as the 
first section. Section  2 discussed the methodological procedures. 
Section  3 described the taxonomy of various PINN architectures. 
Section  4 discussed the application of PINN in hemodynamics. 
The pros and cons of various PINN frameworks and their exper-
imental setup are emphasized in Sections  5 and 6 respectively. 
Section  7 illustrates the results followed by the research chal -
lenges and future research directions in Sections  8 and 9.

3   |   Taxonomy of PINN
The conventional PINN framework faced significant practical 
limitations when used to the complicated multiscale, multi- 
physics cardiovascular hemodynamics. These inherent limita -
tions have led to the development of a taxonomy of specialized 
PINN architectures designed to address specific theoretical and 
practical challenges while maintaining the benefits of physics- 
informed learning. In this section, a critical review of the cur -
rent advanced PINN variants to hemodynamic modeling will be 
done using a systematic evaluation of their technical advances, 
theoretical foundations, implementation challenges, and their 
clinical translation opportunities were assessed. The taxonomy 
of the variants of PINN models were shown in Figure  2.
3.1   |   Roe PINN
Roe PINN integrated the advanced CFD solvers into the PINN 
architectures. This CFD utilized a numerical technique, the 
finite volume method, to solve hyperbolic partial differential 
equations for blood flow. Finite volume method was based on 
the Roe approximate Reimann solver (Roe FV) and involved 
calculating the Roe matrix to linearize the Riemann problem 
at cell interfaces, allowing efficient and accurate flux computa -
tions. Roe PINNs replaced conventional automated differentia -
tion (AD) for PDE residual evaluation, its architecture shown in 
Figure  3. Instead of forming the residual using AD derivatives, 
Roe PINN formed the residual loss by finite volume discretiza -
tion. Uniquely, Roe- PINN incorporated slope limiters, adaptive 
mesh refinement, direct conservative variable prediction, and 
custom activation functions tailored to flow physics.
The core innovation of Roe- PINN laid in the integration of flux- 
difference splitting within the neural network architecture, 
which enabled accurate representation of flow discontinuities 
and shock phenomena that conventional PINNs could not han -
dle effectively. Traditional PINNs approximated the solution 
fields using the Navier–Stokes equations in their differential 
form, which could lead to numerical instabilities and inaccurate 
predictions when dealing with discontinuities. Roe- PINN ad -
dressed this limitation by incorporating flux-  difference splitting 
techniques that enabled the stable and accurate solution of prob-
lems with discontinuities through characteristic decomposition

3.3   |   Warm- Start PINN (WS- PINN)
Warm- start PINNs were developed to overcome the limitations 
of PINN such as difficulty in training for stiff PDEs where opti -
mization becomes unstable, slow convergence by initializing the 
network with prior knowledge, such as approximate numerical 
solver solutions rather than random weights, resulting in accel -
erated convergence with fewer training iterations.
The architectural framework of Warm Start PINN implemented 
a hierarchical curriculum learning strategy that progressively 
increased problem complexity during training, combined with

4   |   Applications of PINN in Hemodynamics
There were inherent problems with non-  invasive measurement 
of arterial pressure. Although CFD could provide highly precise 
numerical solutions based on the governing equations of fluid 
mechanics, its reliance on accurate boundary conditions and the 
time- consuming preprocessing required for solving these equa -
tions limits its applicability in real-  time clinical environments. 
To address these limitations, Du Toit and Laubscher ( 2023) in-
troduced a physics-  based neural-  network model for pressure 
estimation. A model of the aortic arch was constructed using 
idealized human aortic geometrical data and a CFD simulation 
using bidirectional fluid–solid coupling computation was done. 
The spatiotemporal velocity and pressure fields obtained were 
used for the training and validation data of the PINNs. To de -
termine the distribution of velocities and relative pressures, the 
nondimensional Navier–Stokes and continuity equations were 
used in the loss function. Relative pressure was then derived 
using a post- processing step which takes advantage of the linear 
relationship between relative pressure and elastic modulus as 
well as the displacement of the vessel wall.
Isaev et  al.  ( 2024) introduced a unified process involving the 
synthesis of data and advanced neural- network models. The pro-
cess of work began with the construction of three-  dimensional 
meshes of vascular bifurcations with the help of a generator that 
was built on the GMSH library. These meshes were used to per -
form 3D bloodflow simulations, which vary the physiological 
parameters such as, vessel radii, bifurcation angles, inlet/outlet/
pressures, producing a heterogeneous dataset.
PINNs learned the unknown physical phenomena, particularly 
when the equations of the relevant physical systems were im -
plemented into a neural network architecture, thus incorporat -
ing the available data that is held empirically with the help of 
PINNs. Neural networks were also biased to smooth functions, 
and this became difficult to represent cardiovascular flows that 
have a large range of spatial frequencies as seen in the case of 
aneurysms and stenoses. Activation functions based on Fourier- 
based activation functions showed promising reduction in spec -
tral bias (Aghaee and Khan  2024).
The existing technology of the transcranial Doppler ultrasound 
was limited to measuring the velocity of blood at a limited num-
ber of intracranial sites because of the availability of the skull. 
More invasive modalities, such as computed tomography angi -
ography and magnetic resonance pulse-  sequence modalities, 
were invasive and could not be used repeatedly. The suggested 
method combines sparse clinical data with three-  dimensional 
imaging and reduced- order modeling simulations to create high- 
resolution spatiotemporal images of velocity, vessel-  to- vessel 
cross- sectional area, and pressure across cerebral vasculature. 
ASPINN and PINN models have transformer modules and an 
adaptive activation to enforce physical laws and predict hemo -
dynamic evolution throughout the vascular network without 
specifying boundary conditions (Sarabian et al.  2022).
Dynamic contrast-  enhanced magnetic resonance imaging 
(DCE- MRI) had extensive applications in the evaluation of vas -
cularization of tissues especially in cancer settings. Traditional 
pharmacokinetic models often neglected diffusion of the con -
trast agent between the neighboring voxels. As a result, failed 
to yield accurate data in heterogeneous tumors. A PINN-  based 
framework presented by Sainz-  DeMena et  al.  ( 2024) opted for 
measured DCE-  MRI data and included mass-  conservation con-
straints and accounting for inter-  voxel diffusion. The suggested 
approach was compared to some of the algorithms that exist in 
the literature on 1D test cases.
H. J. Ling et  al.  ( 2024) addressed the challenges of modeling 
patient- specific hemodynamics, where initial and boundary 
conditions and model parameters were usually indeterminate 
and highly variable, with the implementation of PINNs based 
on the Navier–Stokes equations. L. Li et al. ( 2024) suggested the 
new direction where the real-  time blood-  pressure information 
fitted through the training of the mappings between the simple 
physiological data to the flow solutions, without the specifica -
tion of an initial or a final condition. The windkessel boundary 
conditions of time periodicity, which combined with a hyper- 
parameter network, allow the transformation of hard model 
coefficients to be estimated at training, which provides physi -
ologically realistic, continuous wave prediction. The presented 
methodology enabled the constant, spatially and temporally 
resolved measurements of blood pressure and velocity through 
wearable devices, thus providing substantial benefits in the 
fields of cardiovascular-  disease identification, as well as, indi -
vidually tailored therapy. The method could forecast complete 
hemodynamic waveforms through space and time which made 
it more clinically applicable.
To improve the accuracy of deep neural networks in smooth and 
discontinuous functions, along with the solutions of linear and 
nonlinear partial differential equations (Jagtap et al.  2020), pro-
posed adaptive activation functions in deep neural networks and 
PINNs. The proposed approach enhanced the performance of 
the network, convergence speed and solution accuracy by add -
ing a trainable hyperparameter to the activation function, espe -
cially at the beginning of the training process. The approach was 
evaluated using classical equations of forward and inverse prob -
lems. Frequency-  domain analysis exhibits effective learning in 
a variety of frequencies. Overall, the approach was shown to be 
more efficient as well as robust and assisted gradient- descent op-
timization to leave suboptimal stationary points and decreased 
loss objectives faster over traditional deep- learning benchmarks 
and different datasets.

Sun et al. ( 2020) adopted a physics- constrained neural- network- 
based approach, which produced mesh-  free surrogate models 
to predict the dynamics of fluid-  flow with great accuracy and

Diffusion Contrast Agents (CA) between neighboring vox -
els often represented in traditional pharmacokinetic models, 
which led to an imprecise heterogeneous tumor environment. 
Although previous algorithms have tried to consider diffusion 
effects, they were often faced with convergence problems be -
cause of the ill-  posed nature of the problem. Sainz-  DeMena 
et al. ( 2024) proposed a PINN- based fitting of dynamic contrast- 
enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (DCE-  MRI) data, where 
the mass-  conservation equation of the pharmacokinetic model 
is imposed, but the CA diffusion is explicitly considered. PINNs 
prove to be better at predicting parameters of vascularization 
using diffusion-  corrected tracer-  kinetic models and behave 
well in the presence of noisy or incomplete data. Therefore, the 
method is an opportunity that can be appreciated as the means 
of even more specific and accurate data related to the DCE-  MRI 
analysis when diffusion effects play an important role.
The forward and inverse problems that characterize blood flow 
dynamics were a determining factor in many biomedical stud -
ies. Their numerical discretization linked to the spatial (Orera 
et  al.  2025) variability of mechanical and geometrical proper -
ties required sophisticated numerical solvers to ensure sta -
bility and proper description of the dynamics of the systems. 
Orera et  al.  ( 2025) presented Roe PINNs, a hybrid model that 
incorporated sophisticated CFD solutions in physics-  informed 
neural networks (PINNs). Orera et al. ( 2025) demonstrated an 
example of a Roe PINNs application to both forward and in -
verse problems involving the propagation of nonlinear waves in 
elastic arteries in the presence of geometric type source terms. 
Orera et al. ( 2025) showed that the augmented Riemann solvers 
could be integrated into the PINN architecture with relatively 
small modifications to its hyperparameters, thus potentially 
being a benefits-  based alternative to automatic differentiation 
(AD), in particular, in the case where the solution had strong 
nonlinearities.
Predictive modeling of cardiovascular flows offers a useful tool in 
cardiovascular monitoring, diagnostics, and surgical planning. 
The advanced methods of computation were highly dependent 
on time-  consuming pre-  processing and calibration steps that 
were normally costly in computation, thus restricting their use 
in the clinical setting. Kissas et al. ( 2020) suggested a machine- 
learning system that could combine a smooth synthesis of non- 
invasive in vivo measurement methods and computational flow 
dynamics models based on first-  principle physical laws. The 
framework guaranteed that the results of deep neural networks 
met the requirements of conservation of mass and momentum 
by demonstrating how to constrain the output of such systems 
using one- dimensional models of pulsatile flow.
Modeling patient- specific hemodynamics using images increas -
ingly used as a diagnostic instrument in a wide range of car -
diovascular conditions. Although the potential of the models to 
improve the diagnostic accuracy and hence clinical outcomes 
was broadly recognized, they typically require extensive compu-
tational resources since most models rely on traditional numer -
ical methods like CFD. In place of these numerical approaches 
(Yevtushenko et al.  2022), presented a machine-  learning (ML)- 
based model for identifying patient-  specific hemodynamic 
parameters. This method had the advantage over CFD-  based 
strategies that compute patient- specific hemodynamic results in 
real time with significantly lower computational effort.
Computational hemodynamic models were crucial in optimis -
ation of surgical plans and understanding of the mechanism of 
cardiovascular diseases. Recent progress in machine-  learning 
methods explored to reduce the computational overhead of 
these models. In an effort to forecast the behavior of blood-  flow 
velocity changes and lumen-  area pulsatile changes along arte -
rial segments (Sen et  al.  2024), suggested using PIGNN along 
with one- dimensional equations of blood-  flow, the dynamics of 
both blood- flow velocity and lumen- area pulse changes. The ap-
proach built a graph that described the topology of the arteries 
and the edges modeled by 1- D lines and the nodes were modeled 
by the points in the flow analysis. The new system decoding 
of mathematical relationships among nodes that produced ve -
locity and lumen pulse waveforms of each node. The empirical 
measurement data used as the basis of the training protocol of 
PIGNNs included inlet and outlet velocity waves and diastolic 
lumen- area measurements in each vessel.
To address challenges associated with integrals in the governing 
equations, A- PINN introduced an Adapted Physics-  Informed 
Neural Network (A- PINN), which using automatic differenti -
ation of these auxiliary outputs instead of traditional integral 
operators (Yuan et al.  2022). In contrast to the original PINN, 
which only provides approximations of variables in the govern -
ing equations, the modified framework builds a multi-  output 
neural network that can at the same time approximate primary 
outputs as well as auxiliary outputs estimating both the variables 
and the integrals. Thereafter, the connection between primary 
and auxiliary outputs is limited by newly brought output con -
ditions imposing adherence to physical laws (Yuan et al.  2022).
Recent studies have examined PINN architectures to learn to 
simulate the hemodynamics by combining mathematical mod -
els of blood flow with empirical data much faster. A variable- 
separated PINN, which was grounded on adaptive weighted loss 
functions (AW- vsPINN), had been created in order to model 
blood flow in arteries. In this architecture, the sub-  neural 
networks were set up to produce predictions of unknown sca -
lar state variables when shared a common input layer (Jagtap 
et  al.  2020). The Adaptive Weighted PINN (AWsPINN) opti -
mally tuned the weights in the loss term through a minmax 
optimization paradigm; the weights were adjusted alongside

PINNs have been used to predict blood flow by coded nonlinear 
Navier–Stokes equations and training data into the neural net -
work framework. Non- invasive, real- time, and accurate acquisi-
tion of hemodynamic variables was a problem that the existing 
invasive detection and simulation algorithms faced. To achieve 
prediction of the velocity, pressure, and WSS without using sim-
ulation data, Liu et al. ( 2025) suggested an integral conservation 
physics- informed neural network (ICPINN) that used adaptive 
activation functions to predict the quantities since they were 
dependent on patient-  specific vessel geometries. To obtain un -
supervised learning, the loss function used mass- flow- rate resid-
uals of the mass- conservation law which significantly improved 
the accuracy and efficiency of the predictions (Liu et al.  2025).
Near- wall blood flow and WSS had a central role in the patho -
genesis of significant cardiovascular diseases. However, ac -
curate measurement of WSS in  vivo remains challenging. 
Computational and experimental measurements of WSS 
tailored to the patient have weaknesses in uncertainty, low 
resolution, and noise. PINNs offered a highly adaptable deep- 
learning framework with the capability to combine the equa -
tions governing blood flow with measurement data. PINNs did 
not have the large-  data bottleneck associated with deep learn -
ing because of the understanding of the governing equations. 
Arzani et al. ( 2021) established that a PINN had the potential to 
enhance WSS measurements of diseased arteries without issues 
with unknown inlet and outlet boundary conditions by convert -
ing very few measurement points. One of the most typical char -
acteristics of patient- specific CFD models was the uncertainty of 
boundary conditions, and (Arzani et al.  2021) also demonstrated 
that PINNs utilized the sparse measurements of velocity further 
out of the wall to measure WSS with extremely high accuracy 
without knowing full information about the wall conditions.
In order to simulate blood circulation in the coronary arteries, 
the PINN-  CFD hybrid framework for coronary hemodynam -
ics proposed a methodology that integrates deep learning with 
the general laws of physics. Their methodology was effective in 
predicting fractional flow reserve (FFR) by integrating physics- 
based models with clinical data as this enables them to over -
come the difficulties of non- invasive measurements. The quality 
and effectiveness of the suggested model were supported by the 
validation on CFD and invasive fractional flow reserve (FFR) 
methods (Alzhanov et al.  2024).
Training PINNs for specific flow was usually more time- 
consuming than in traditional CFD methods, which hampered 
their extensive use. In both strategies explored, a multi-  case 
parameterized PINN approach was proposed where the differ -
ent geometry cases were parameterized and pre-  trained on the 
PINN (Wong et al.  2024). First, additional inputs to the PINN, 
like distance to the wall and distance to the centerline, were the 
coordinate parameters depending on tube geometry that could 
improve the accuracy and minimize the computational load of 
the model at a significant level. Second, the use of additional 
loss terms was applied to impose zero derivatives of the existing 
physics constraints on the PINN and hypernetwork, enhanc -
ing the performance of the mixed network and hypernetwork 
(Wong et al.  2024).
In many cases, discovering the hemodynamic models from 
macroscopic measurements was inherently non- unique. Many 
pairs of possibles and operators could produce an identical set 
of macroscopic dynamics, which was a barrier to physical in -
terpretation of the learnt models. To solve this non- uniqueness 
problem, Statistical- Physics- Informed Neural Networks (Stat- 
PINNs) introduced a machine-  learning framework that incor -
porates knowledge of statistical mechanics (Huang et al.  2025). 
The framework had been formulated in dissipative isothermal 
systems. Interestingly, it employed data from short time particle 
simulations to learn the thermodynamic structure that could be 
used to predict long time macroscopic evolutions and method -
ology to the dynamics of particle systems with Arrhenius-  type 
interaction and included a wide range of phenomena including 
diffusion of defects in solid materials, surface absorption, and 
chemical reactions (Huang et al.  2025).
Aortic dissection (AD) was a life- threatening condition that con-
tributes significantly to morbidity and mortality in the strands of 
different ages. Hemodynamic variables measured quantitatively 
in aneurysmal and dissected aortas have increased the prognos -
tic data in AD and aortic aneurysms (Daneker et al.  2024). To 
achieve better results, warm-  start physics-  informed neural net -
works (WS- PINNs) have been proposed that allowed training 
through transfer learning and physics. WS- PINNs reduced the 
reliance on large-  scale measurements and reduced the effects 
of prescribed inflow/outflow boundary conditions on predictive 
accuracy, which provided better predictive performance in clin -
ical decision- making. The framework was promising in the ap -
plication to other aortic pathologies such as arterial stenosis and 
other types of aneurysms (Daneker et al.  2024).
Artificial-  intelligence- based physiological monitoring on wear -
able devices had great potential to create precise medicine. 
However, traditional data-  driven AI paradigms need a lot of 
ground- truth training data to predict complex cardiovascular 
parameters. In biomedical applications, it was difficult, tedious, 
and impossible to collect large amounts of personalized ground- 
truth data, making these methods impractical in the context 
of widespread, personalized healthcare implementation (Sel 
et al.  2023). To address this limitation, a PINN- based framework 
for cuffless blood pressure estimation continuous cuffless blood 
pressure using minimum ground-  truth. The major innovation 
was the addition of Taylor series approximations to the train -
ing of the neural networks, hence including known, gradually 
developing associations among sensor inputs and cardiovascu -
lar outputs. The physics-  based loss term, which was based on 
partial differential equation proposed approximations, imposed 
known dynamics and relations between variables even in  sit -
uations where direct supervisory information was thin and 
thereby diminished the amount of training information needed 
(Sel et al.  2023).

obtaining non-  invasive, real-  time measurement of the patient-

the existing invasive detection and simulation algorithms. J.-  X.

Numerical modeling of industrial flows based on the Reynolds- 
averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) equations relied on turbulence 
modeling. Although, Generalized RANS models were predictive 
and strong enough to be used. The major constraint to model 
accuracy with large disparities in the RANS-  predicted Reynolds 
stresses. In order to resolve these discrepancies, suggested that 
data- driven, physics-  informed machine-  learning should be 
used to retrieve the discrepancies in RANS-  modeled Reynolds 
stresses. The discrete formulations were developed as functions 
of mean flow characteristics and developed by random- forest al-
gorithm on available DNS databases, which were subsequently 
applied to predict the Reynolds stresses. The offered approach is 
tested on the two types of flows. a fully developed turbulent flow 
in a square duct at different Reynolds numbers, and flows with 
strong separation (J.- X. Wang et al.  2017).
Intraventricular vector flow mapping (VFM) was a recently 
used echocardiographic modality that generated time-  resolved 
two- dimensional flow maps in the left ventricle (LV) using color- 
Doppler sequences. The existing models of VFM were based 
on kinematic limits that were caused by planar-  flow incom -
pressibility without incorporating any important information 
of flow-  physics, especially the pressure and shear stresses and 
the corresponding accelerations. This shortcoming negatively 
affected the ability of VFM to aggregate data over time or to gen-
erate pressure maps at varying times. Maidu et al. ( 2025) took 
advantage of the recent developments of artificial intelligence 
to create AI- VFM a visual-  flow- modeling modality that implic -
itly included PINNs to recover mass conservation and momen -
tum conservation at the left ventricle (LV) and impose no-  slip 
boundary conditions at the left ventricle endocardium. AI-  VFM 
recovered the flow and pressure fields of the LV using regular 
echocardiographic images.
PINNs have already proven promising applications in a wide 
range of engineering fields, especially in the management of 
noisy data and inverse problems that were modeled by partial 
differential equations (PDEs). However, PINN not completely 
proven that they were effective when it comes to predicting any 
nonlinear phenomenon in multi-  physics regimes, particularly 
when it comes to predicting turbulence. Shukla et al. ( 2025) pro-
posed a Neuro SEM hybrid architecture that combined PINNs 
and the high-  fidelity Spectral Element Method (SEM) solver 
(Nektar++). Neuro SEM utilized the strengths of PINNs and 
SEM, providing strong solutions to multi-  physics problems. In 
this context, PINNs learned data and physical processes on par -
ticular subdomains and then were used in Nektar++.
In the cavopulmonary circulation created by the Fontan proce -
dure, Dobroserdova et al. ( 2024) suggested a new methodology, 
which utilized Physics-  Informed Neural Network (PINN) as a 
junction condition of a one-  dimensional blood-  flow network 
model. The scheme had a complex, physically regular geometry 
of a neural network and a three-  dimensional mesh-  generation 
scheme based on junction-  geometry parameterization. Also, 
synthetic costumes were produced through the 3D steady-  state 
Stokes- flow simulations with prescribed boundary conditions to 
validate the method.
Existing MRI diffusion methodologies, particularly two-  
compartment incoherent motion (IVIM) models, were not de -
tailed enough in describing the complex micro-  architecture of 
the brain. Such strategies lack the ability to solve a series of 
diffusion elements such as the interstitial fluid (ISF), which 
was relevant to cerebrovascular and neurodegenerative dis -
eases (Voorter et  al.  2023). The physics-  informed neural 
network (PINN) architecture had been used to fit a three-  
component IVIM (3C-  IVIM) model to multispectral b- value 
diffusion MRI data. PINNs fitted to estimate three diffusion 
components, namely parenchymal diffusion, intermediate ISF 
diffusion, and microvascular pseudo-  diffusion, using six par -
allel neural sub-  networks that learned the necessary model 
parameters.
A new hybrid computational framework integrated PINNs with 
CFD and FSI models to simulate a stenotic coronary artery tree 
and predict fractional flow reserve (FFR), a key diagnostic pa -
rameter of coronary stenosis, in the context of simulating hae -
modynamic flow (Alzhanov et  al.  2024). The core component 
of this framework was a one-  dimensional PINN that predicted 
outflow boundary conditions; therefore, it eliminated the chal -
lenges related to direct measurement or estimation of boundary 
conditions in complicated vascular systems. The approach to 
predict the flow with high precision by imposing physical laws 
into the PINN loss and by introducing generated initial condi -
tions into full FSI flow simulations produced realistic and phys -
iologically compliant flow predictions that were accurate across 
CFD benchmarks.

Cardiovascular assessment heavily relied on accurate haemody -
namic analysis. However, the classical CFD models were com -
putationally expensive and had not support transfer learning. 
In contrast, the classical machine-  learning models require la -
beled data which was scarcely available in clinical practice. The 
limitations were mitigated by PINNs, which included physical 
laws in the loss function to obtain strong modeling in a vari -
ety of geometries. However, traditional PINNs tend to be less 
effective in areas with a complicated vessel outline, including 
stenoses, and thus their efficiency and accuracy were reduced. 
To address this, Boundary Progressive PINN (BP- PINN) frame -
work had proposed. In this framework, X. Chen et al. ( 2025) in-
troduced the adaptive complexity of boundary, redeveloping of 
vascular boundaries with different smoothness to improve flow 
characterization.
Intracranial aneurysms (IAS) were associated with a high prob -
ability of rupture, and they required accurate haemodynamic 
assessment in their successful clinical management. Kim and 
Koo ( 2025) examined the use of PINNs as a CFD- free solution to 
simulate the velocity of blood flow, pressure, and WSS inside the 
aortic wall. The PINN forecasted velocity fields, flow vectors, 
pressure, and WSS using inputs of three-  dimensional and two- 
dimensional domains and reproduced several important physi -
ological characteristics that included the reduction of velocity in 
the aneurysm and the high WSS in the aneurysm neck.
The accurate modeling of self- gravitating gaseous flows was the 
fundamental issue in the field of astrophysics, affecting a variety 
of phenomena, including planetary and stellar formation, gal -
axy and large-  scale structure formation processes. The primary 
challenge lied in solving of highly nonlinear, time-  dependent, 
three- dimensional partial differential equations (PDEs) that 
represented the combined action of gravity and fluid dynam -
ics. Using their universal and mesh free formulation as well as 
property of approximation of functions in PINNs, represented 
a new opportunity to tackle these complicated systems at scale. 
To simulate three-  dimensional self-  gravitating hydrodynamics 
with a PINN, Auddy et al. ( 2024) suggest a architecture named 
GRINN. GRINN had been found to predict gravitational in -
stabilities and wave propagation in isothermal gases and has a 
high fidelity, in the linear regime and in the nonlinear regime. 
One notable benefit of this method was computational scaling, 
whereas conventional grid-  based solvers of PDEs will grow ex -
ponentially in cost with the dimensions added, GRINN essen -
tially scaled runtime independent of dimensions and especially 
efficient in simulations of three dimensions.
The accurate characterization of the infiltration of glioblastoma 
(GBM) based on the medical MRI data was essential for under -
standing the tumor dynamics and personalized radiotherapy 
planning. Mathematical reaction–diffusion models were use -
ful supplements to imaging data because they predicted the lo -
calisation of tumor cells (X. Zhang et  al.  2023). Nevertheless, 
patient- specific model parameters were difficult to determine 
using clinical data, and the task was difficult because patients 
were limited in terms of time resolution and sparse informa -
tion. To overcome this issue, a PINN- based parameter inference 
glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) growth model was proposed 
to predict patient-  specific parameters based on a single, three- 
dimensional structural MRI scan. The framework combined 
theoretical knowledge with clinical data as both the PDE and 
clinical data were incorporated into the loss function of the neu-
ral network (X. Zhang et al.  2023). The major advances were the 
discovery and rough determination of non-  dimensional model 
parameters, pre-  training using model parameters, fine-  tuning 
personal patient inferences, and the use of a diffuse domain 
method in the PINN to precisely approximate complex cerebral 
geometries (X. Zhang et al.  2023).
PINNs were a novel class of machine learning algorithms that 
were able to solve parameterised problems in parallel with 
high efficiency, resulting in favorable computational scaling. 
However, the method was still in the early stage of development, 
and a number of open challenges were still present. Training dy-
namics tend to be numerically stiff; powerful mechanisms for re-
solving turbulent flows were still scarce, and the optimal choice 
of hyperparameters were unclear. Du Toit and Laubscher ( 2023) 
approach the research systematically to examine the accuracy 
and efficiency of PINNs to simulate the transvascular blood 
flow in the aorta in both laminar and turbulent flow conditions, 
with various advanced literature-  based methods to make the 
simulations as realistic as possible.
In echocardiography, the intraventricular vector flow mapping 
(IVFM) used to approximate two-  dimensional blood flow veloc-
ity fields in the left ventricle using color Doppler ultrasound im -
ages. However, the traditional IVFM approach had significant 
drawbacks. It was based on a least-  squares optimisation sub -
ject to a limited set of regularization parameters and required 
explicit boundary conditions, which were often challenging to 
obtain clinically. Moreover, the method was computationally in-
tensive and not capable of real- time implementation. To address 
this issue, PINNs for intraventricular flow reconstruction pro -
posed a framework based on PINNs in governing fluid- dynamics 
equations that were learnable through the neural-  network loss, 
since radial and angular velocity components could be learned 
directly by looking at Doppler data (H. J. Ling et  al.  2024). 
There were two types of such a framework, RB- PINNs based on 
adaptive loss balancing through dynamic penalty coefficients, 
and AL- PINNs (Augmented Lagrangian) based on the use of 
Lagrange multipliers to strengthen constraint satisfaction (H. J. 
Ling et al.  2024).
Mechanics of blood-  flow in microcirculation were important 
to understand the physiology of vascular health and diseases 
like diabetic retinopathy. However, in  vivo imaging processes 
have not succeeded in resolving instantaneous 3D flow in small 
vessels; existing in vitro methods such as micro- Particle Image 
Velocimetry, optical flow or CFD approaches have serious lim -
itations in determining velocity and stress fields in small vessels 
with complicated geometries (Wong et  al.  2025). Specifically, 
CFD methods were based on assumed boundary conditions that 
leave significant uncertainty, and there was no significant meth-
odology available to reliably infer full 3D hemodynamic fields 
(velocity, pressure, WSS) using limited 2D experimental data.

8   |   Research Challenges
PINNs were commonly used for solving complex partial differ -
ential equations (PDEs) in hemodynamic flow and vascular me -
chanics that intrinsically generate unsteady learning dynamics 
and slow convergence. Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) provided 
a learning process based on the induced kernel of the network 
Jacobian. As a result, such networks became responsive to 
sharp gradients, layers of shear, and transitional or turbulent 
flows (J.- X. Wang et al.  2017). The above weakness was further 
compounded by the spectral bias; traditional coordinate-  based 
Multilayer Perceptrons (MLPs) exhibit a bias toward low fre -
quency representations. Thus, limiting their ability to capture 
near- wall gradients and small-  scale vortical structures. In addi -
tion, NTK generally made the assumptions of loss and ignored 
the effects of boundary conditions and multi-  physics couplings, 
which were inherent to the biofluid problems.
Physics informed neural networks used adaptive activation 
functions which made gradient pathologies to reduce in highly 
nonlinear regimes. However, optimization was a difficult task 
(Jagtap et al.  2020). Consequently, it continued to be a tedious 
task to train PINNs on realistic cardiovascular flows. Wong 
et  al.  ( 2024) investigated the implementation of multi-  case 
PINNs to turbulent flows and found that optimization chal -
lenges grow exponentially with the complexity of the flow, thus 
requiring a large amount of hyper-  parameter optimization and 
long- term training.
Progressive boundary-  complexity hemodynamics model PINN 
application was a modeling method that adds boundary condi -
tions steadily to improve training stability and solution accuracy 
(X. Chen et al.  2025). In spite of these developments, it was still 
not feasible to enforce physiologically realistic boundary condi -
tions, including pulsatile flow and compliant vessel walls, which 
have led to problem- specific ad- hoc adaptations, thus restricting 
the cases of PINN models in biofluid dynamics.
GRINN model demonstrated the multi-  scale complexity that 
became difficult to represent in a single PINN architecture 
in the situation of hydrodynamic systems, which include self- 
gravity (Auddy et al.  2024). The governing equations that were 
related to the problem were often stiff, which slowed down the 
convergence and causes numerical instability. Moreover, the dy-
namics of blood flow were non-  smooth with sudden changes in 
the flow variables and vessel elasticity, which made the learn -
ing process a challenge for physics-  informed neural operators. 
Computational costs were further enhanced by the high dimen -
sionality of the problem of vascular flow and the need to resolve

9   |   Future Research Directions
The next- generation PINN implementation derived to be beyond 
the conventional settings of a non- learning heuristics of optimi -
zation optimistic techniques. It included the use of meta learned 
optimizers, which were automatically tuning the training pro -
cess by PDE residuals and the stiffness variations between the 
domains. Furthermore, physics-  informed regularization could 
be used to decrease the oscillations of the solution predicted.
Modeling high frequency flow features using the NTK urging for 
more advanced architectures. Neural operators could be used to 
train for more complicated problems in fluid dynamics. Future 
architectures are expected to make use of random expansions 
based on the eigenfunctions of the linearized Navier–Stokes 
operator, and thus bring the inductive bias of the network into 
agreement with the natural spectral properties of the underlying 
partial differential equation.

synthetic vascular data.
Quantification of uncertainty was one of the most important 
directions of refining hemodynamic PINN models. Stochastic 
differential equations were used in PINN where the net -
work parameters are developed according to probabilistic 
dynamics. Another promising direction is the development 
of Bayesian PINNs, which aim to estimate the posterior dis -
tribution of solutions in high-  dimensional function spaces 
while maintaining computational efficiency. Besides, hybrid 
probabilistic–deterministic models integrating the synergistic 
Normalizing Flows with Gaussian Processes are positioned 
to provide well-  calibrated uncertainty estimates. These ap -
proaches will be able to increase the accuracy of PINN fore -
casts of hemodynamics.
10   |   Conclusion
Physics- informed neural network plays a vital role in the field 
of hemodynamics. Many models have been introduced to ad -
dress the problems in hemodynamics. In this review, we pro -
vide a systematic analysis of PINN models in the hemodynamics 
and classify the models into nine taxonomies based on the ad -
vancements in terms of computational cost, boundary progres -
sion, and clinical use. The numerical evaluation indicated that 
the PIGNN models showed precise and highly accurate pre -
dictions even when limited data were provided. However, the 
performance remained sensitive to the boundary conditions. 
Moreover, we critically examined the most recent PINN models, 
assessing them in terms of the advantages and drawbacks in the 
field of hemodynamics. We also discussed the existing short -
comings and the unsolved problems that exist in current models 
of PINN as well as outline promising prospects in their further 
development. Despite several challenges, PINN-  based models 
have consistently delivered impressive results.
